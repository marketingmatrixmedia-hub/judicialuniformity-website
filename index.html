Are Georgia's Magistrate Court clerks following O.C.G.A. § 24-13-21(c)?

The statute reads: The clerk of court shall make subpoenas in blank available on demand by electronic or other means to parties or their counsel or to the grand jury. It sets no hearing-date, motion, or judicial-approval condition. The Court of Appeals has held that "shall" in the predecessor statute is a word of command (State v. Brantley, 147 Ga. App. 569 (1978)).

This page documents, county by county, published court instructions that add conditions the statute does not contain. Every quote is the county's own published wording, with a preserved copy of the source and an independent Internet Archive capture.

Quotes re-verified against live sources 2026-11-26  ·  first captured 2026-07-28

Why this matters

  • The duty is mandatory. Section 24-13-21(c) says the clerk "shall" make blank subpoenas available "on demand" to "parties or their counsel." A clerk's refusal to perform a mandatory duty may be compelled by mandamus in superior court (O.C.G.A. § 9-6-20).
  • It decides whether evidence reaches the hearing. A litigant who cannot get a subpoena until a hearing is set, or until a judge grants a motion, may lose the chance to compel witnesses or obtain records before they are gone.
  • It falls hardest on people without lawyers. The statute covers "parties or their counsel" alike. A self-represented litigant who is told to wait, file a motion, or come back later has no attorney to push back.
  • These are published court instructions, not one clerk's mistake. Each county below states its condition on its own official page as the court's procedure. Under their own preamble, the Uniform Magistrate Court Rules "shall yield to substantive law," and a local practice cannot add conditions to a statute.
